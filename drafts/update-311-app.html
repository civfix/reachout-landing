Reach Out LA receives $10,000 to build a better 311 app

A one-time donation will fund Civfix, a third-party 311 app that is easier to use in any city and shows how long reports take to get fixed in richer and poorer neighborhoods.

By Reach Out LA · Published May 19, 2026

Updated November 12, 2026: added new research from Philadelphia.

Reach Out Los Angeles has received a one-time donation of $10,000 to build Civfix, our third-party 311 app: a single, easy way for residents anywhere in the country to report problems like illegal dumping, broken streetlights and potholes, and to see what happens next.

What we’re building

Today, most cities run their own 311 systems, with different apps, categories and rules. Our goal is one app that works the same way wherever you live, built around a simple promise: you should always be able to see what happened to your report.

Civfix will also make government responsiveness visible. It will track how long reports take to be resolved, broken down by the average household income of the neighborhood where each report was filed, so residents, reporters and city leaders can see whether every neighborhood is getting the same service.

Why response times need daylight

Studies disagree about how big the gaps are and why they exist, but several have found that lower-income neighborhoods wait longer for city services.

In Philadelphia, an analysis of about 466,000 311 requests from 2024 and 2025 found that the median lower-income ZIP code waited roughly 54 percent longer than the median higher-income ZIP code. The gap held even for the same kinds of requests: abandoned-vehicle complaints took about 150 days to close in lower-income ZIP codes versus about 135 in higher-income ones, and broken alley lights about 65 days versus about 45.1 The city disputes that income plays a role, pointing to neighborhood factors such as extra services downtown.

A 2024 study of about 42 million 311 requests across 13 big U.S. cities found that whiter, wealthier neighborhoods do get faster responses on average. It also found that cities don’t answer the same type of request faster in those neighborhoods; the gap mostly comes from poorer neighborhoods reporting problems that take longer to fix.2

Where a gap exists, residents feel it the same way, whatever the cause. Whether it comes from bias, from the kinds of problems a neighborhood faces, or from where resources go, nobody can tell without the numbers, and most cities don’t publish them by neighborhood income. We want to change that.

What residents say about 311 today

“Closed” often isn’t “fixed.” When New York’s City Council looked at 2018 data, about a quarter of 311 complaints were closed because the agency “did not observe” the problem, and nearly one in five because no action was taken.3 In San Antonio, residents found complaints marked closed while the problem was still there; one told reporters, “I felt lied to.” The city’s 311 director acknowledged that closed doesn’t necessarily mean resolved.4

The apps can get in the way. After Los Angeles redesigned MyLA311, residents and neighborhood councils reported location errors, invalid addresses, missing categories and requests routed to the wrong area, sometimes outside the city. One neighborhood council said the new app made it harder to request services. In April 2026, the Los Angeles City Council voted 12–0 to require regular reports on backlogs and average response times.5

What’s next

We’ll share progress on this page as we build. If you want to help test the app, or you have a 311 story of your own, we’d love to hear from you.
